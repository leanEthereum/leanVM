\section{Bus interactions}\label{sec:omc}

The machine runs four interactions on the bus, differentiated by their domain separators (\S\ref{sec:m3}): the VM state, the registers, the memory (RAM and the advice, which share a separator and never share an address), and the public bytecode. All but the first use offline memory checking~\cite{BEGKN94}: the three read-write arrays ordered by a clock (\S\ref{sec:memchan}), and the read-only bytecode in the simpler form of \S\ref{sec:lookup}.

Two encodings of an integer $i$ coexist on the bus. What the machine indexes (a register, a memory cell, an instruction), what it holds (a register's value, an address) and when it does so (a clock, a timestamp) is the integer itself, $\intk{i}$ (\S\ref{sec:vm}). What the proof system only ever steps by one (a read count) is the power $\gen^{i}$, whose successor is a free multiplication by $\gen$. Nothing adds two integers on the bus: every address a row names is a field of its bytecode entry, a word its circuit computed, or an XOR (\S\ref{sec:exp}), and every clock a row pushes is its own clock XOR a word its clock circuit computed (\S\ref{sec:clock}).

\subsection{VM State}\label{sec:state}

\paragraph{Clocks.} A \emph{clock} is an integer below $2^{41}$, read as the element of $\K$ with those bits:
\[
  \intk{2^{40}\ell+2^{5}c+k},\qquad \ell\in\{0,1\},\quad c<2^{35},\quad k<2^{5}.
\]
Bit $40$, $\ell$, is the \emph{live} bit: every clock of the run has it, and so does every seed's timestamp (\S\ref{sec:memchan}), while a padding row's clock is $\intk{0}$ (\S\ref{sec:e2e-pad}). The bits $5$ to $39$ count the cycle $c$, and the five low ones are the slot $k$: zero in a row's clock, and the slot of an access in its timestamp, which is therefore $\ts\oplus\intk{k}=\ts+\intk{k}$, a column plus a constant.

The state interaction uses domain separator $\dsep{ST} = \gen^{0}$ and carries the program counter, clock, and an exit marker. Each row pulls $\tup{\dsep{ST},\pc,\ts,0}$ and pushes $\tup{\dsep{ST},\pc',\ts',\exitflag\,\ts'}$, where $\exitflag$ is the bytecode's exit selector, $\ts'=\ts+\mathit{step}$, and $\mathit{step}$ is a word of the row's clock circuit (\S\ref{sec:clock}). That circuit makes $\ts'$ the clock one cycle on, $\intk{(2^{40}\ell+2^{5}c+2^{5}\ell)\bmod2^{41}}$, when the row's accesses are in order, so that a live clock advances and any other stays put, and sets bit $41$ of $\ts'$ when one of them is not. Only \texttt{ecall} has $\exitflag=1$.

The boundary tuples are
\begin{align*}
  \sigma_{\mathrm{initial}} &= \tup{\dsep{ST},\intk{\pc_{\mathrm{entry}}},\intk{2^{40}+2^{5}},0},\\
  \sigma_{\mathrm{final}} &= \tup{\dsep{ST},\intk{\tbase+4(\nprog-1)},\tsfinal,\tsfinal}.
\end{align*}
The run starts on cycle $1$, and the verifier requires $\tsfinal$ to be a live clock at slot $0$: bit $40$ set, every higher bit and the five low ones clear. Thus only an exit row can meet the final tuple: a jump to the halt address is insufficient. A padding row's marker carries no live clock and cannot end the run, nor can a row whose accesses are out of order.

\begin{proposition}[Balance implies the existence of a valid execution]\label{prop:walk}
Write $\sigma_{\mathrm{initial}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{final}}$ for the two boundary states. Let $\mathcal R$ be a finite multiset of rows, each pulling a state $\bpull(r)$ and pushing a state $\bpush(r)$, and suppose the state channel balances:
\[
  \mset{\bpush(r):r\in\mathcal R}\uplus\mset{\sigma_{\mathrm{initial}}}
  \;=\;\mset{\bpull(r):r\in\mathcal R}\uplus\mset{\sigma_{\mathrm{final}}} .
\]
Then $\mathcal R$ splits into rows $r_1,\dots,r_L$ with
\[
  \bpull(r_1)=\sigma_{\mathrm{initial}},\qquad \bpush(r_i)=\bpull(r_{i+1}),\qquad \bpush(r_L)=\sigma_{\mathrm{final}},
\]
and rows forming closed walks.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Build the directed multigraph $G$ on the states: one edge $\bpull(r)\to \bpush(r)$ per row, plus one edge $e$ from $\sigma_{\mathrm{final}}$ to $\sigma_{\mathrm{initial}}$.

Fix a state $x$. Its in-degree in $G$ counts the rows with $\bpush(r)=x$, plus $e$ if $x=\sigma_{\mathrm{initial}}$. Its out-degree counts the rows with $\bpull(r)=x$, plus $e$ if $x=\sigma_{\mathrm{final}}$. Those two counts are the multiplicities of $x$ on the two sides of the hypothesis, so they are equal: $G$ is balanced at every state.

A finite balanced multigraph is a union of edge-disjoint closed walks: follow unused edges from any vertex, which balance always permits on entering one, until a vertex repeats; remove the closed walk found and repeat. Deleting $e$ from the closed walk that contains it leaves $r_1,\dots,r_L$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The clock tells the run from the rest]\label{cor:clock}
In Proposition~\ref{prop:walk}, let every row pull a clock below $2^{41}$ and push the clock its clock circuit gives, and let $\sigma_{\mathrm{initial}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{final}}$ be as above. Then every row's accesses are in order, the rows whose clock is live are exactly $r_1,\dots,r_L$, the clock of $r_i$ being $\intk{2^{40}+2^{5}i}$, so that $L<2^{35}$ and these clocks are pairwise distinct, and every row on a closed walk has a clock without the live bit.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
A row whose accesses are not in order pushes a clock with bit $41$ set. No row pulls it, and neither does $\sigma_{\mathrm{final}}$, whose clock is below $2^{41}$, so by balance there is no such row. Every other row pushes its own clock if it lacks the live bit, so a walk that meets a clock without the live bit never leaves them. A row with a live clock pushes the clock one cycle on, live again unless the cycle wraps, from $2^{35}-1$ to a clock of $0$ with the live bit clear.

Along $r_1,\dots,r_L$ the clock starts live, on cycle $1$, and ends on the live $\tsfinal$, so it never loses the live bit, which it could not regain. Each $r_i$ is therefore live, the cycle never wraps, and the clock of $r_i$ is $\intk{2^{40}+2^{5}i}$ with $i<2^{35}$. Around a closed walk, either some clock lacks the live bit, and then all of them do, or every clock is live, the cycle never wraps, and the integers the clocks name increase strictly around the walk, which is absurd.
\end{proof}

No bound on the number of rows enters the corollary: a run of $2^{35}-1$ cycles or more would wrap its clock to $0$ and never reach a live final clock, so it simply has no proof, and an honest prover refuses one. One commitment holds far fewer rows anyway: every row commits at least one word of its own, and the witness has at most $2^{28}$ words (\S\ref{sec:e2e-unrolled}).

\begin{remark}
  Closed walks of clocks without the live bit are what lets a table be filled to a power of two (\S\ref{sec:e2e-pad}): a padding row's clock is $\intk{0}$, which it pushes back, so such a row can share nothing with the run, neither a state nor, as \S\ref{sec:memchan} shows, a memory tuple.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The lookup protocol}\label{sec:lookup}

We use a similar idea to~\cite[Protocol.~4.27]{DP23}.

\paragraph{Setting.} A \emph{lookup array} $\lut$ is read-only and holds $2^{\kappa}$ entries, entry $\lut[i]$ being a tuple of $\ncomp$ elements of $\K$, possibly none ($\ncomp=0$). Its \emph{components} $\lut_0,\dots,\lut_{\ncomp-1}$ are the multilinears of height $2^{\kappa}$ holding one coordinate of the entry each: they are either committed or public. Entry $i$ sits at a public address $\addr_i\in\K$, the $2^{\kappa}$ addresses pairwise distinct: the integer $\intk{\tbase+4i}$ for the bytecode (\S\ref{sec:bytecode}). A \emph{read} is a row of a table (\S\ref{sec:m3}) naming an address $a\in\K$ and a value $v\in\K^{\ncomp}$, and is correct when $a=\addr_i$ and $v=\lut[i]$ for some $i<2^{\kappa}$.

\paragraph{Tuple and columns.} The bus interaction uses a tuple of the form $\tup{\sep,\addr,\cnt,\val}$: a domain separator naming the array, the address, the count, then the entry in coordinates $3,\dots,\ncomp+2$. Every higher coordinate is zero, so $\ncomp\le m-3$ for the bus width $m$ (\S\ref{sec:m3}). Take every part of a read to be a committed $\K$-valued column of the reading table: its address, its count, and the $\ncomp$ components of the value read. Committed on top of $\lut$ is one finalize-count $\K$-valued column $\cntfin$ of height $2^{\kappa}$.

\paragraph{Flush rules.} With $A[i]$ the number of reads of address $\addr_i$:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Seed:} for each $i<2^{\kappa}$, \push\ $\tup{\sep,\addr_i,1,\lut[i]}$. A boundary block owned by no table, its address coordinate one of the index columns of \S\ref{sec:idxcol}.
\item \textbf{Read} of $v$ at $a$ with count $\cnt$: \pull\ $\tup{\sep,a,\cnt,v}$ and \push\ $\tup{\sep,a,\gen\cdot\cnt,v}$, both flushes of the reading row.
\item \textbf{Finalize:} for each $i$, \pull\ $\tup{\sep,\addr_i,\cntfin[i],\lut[i]}$. An honest prover commits $\cntfin[i]=\gen^{A[i]}$, the count the address has reached.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Completeness.} An honest prover gives the $t$-th read of $\addr_i$ the count $\gen^{t}$ and commits $\cntfin[i]=\gen^{A[i]}$: both sides then carry the counts $\gen^{0},\dots,\gen^{A[i]}$ at that address, so the bus balances.

\begin{lemma}[Invariant count multisets]\label{lem:invcnt}
Let $\rcnts$ be a finite multiset over $\K$ with $\gen\cdot\rcnts=\rcnts$. If $|\rcnts|<2^{64}-1$, every element of $\rcnts$ is $0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $\mathrm{mult}:\K\to\mathbb{N}$ be the multiplicity function of $\rcnts$ (Definition~\ref{def:multiset}). Multiplication by $\gen$ is a bijection of $\K$ and $\gen\cdot\rcnts$ has multiplicity function $c\mapsto\mathrm{mult}(\gen^{-1}c)$, so the hypothesis reads $\mathrm{mult}(\gen c)=\mathrm{mult}(c)$ for all $c$. Every nonzero $c$ is a power of $\gen$, so iterating this gives one value $\mathrm{mult}_1$ on all of $\K^{\times}$, which has $2^{64}-1$ elements. Then $|\rcnts|=\mathrm{mult}(0)+\mathrm{mult}_1(2^{64}-1)<2^{64}-1$ forces $\mathrm{mult}_1=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Lookup correctness]\label{thm:omc}
Let the flush rules above be the only flushes carrying $\lut$'s separator, and write $(a_j,c_j,v_j)$ for the address, count and value that read $j$ evaluates to. If the bus balances, every $c_j\neq0$, and there are fewer than $2^{64}-1$ reads, then every read is correct: $(a_j,v_j)=(\addr_i,\lut[i])$ for some $i<2^{\kappa}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix a read pair $(a,v)$ that is \emph{not} an entry of the array, so $(a,v)\neq(\addr_i,\lut[i])$ for every $i<2^{\kappa}$. Balance equates the multiset of pushed tuples with the multiset of pulled ones; keep in each only the tuples carrying $\lut$'s separator, address $a$ and entry $v$, and the two are still equal. A seed and a finalization sit at the pairs $(\addr_i,\lut[i])$, different from $(a,v)$, so neither survives. A read at $(a,v)$ does, on both sides at once: it pulls its count $c$ and pushes $\gen c$. So the survivors are exactly the reads at $(a,v)$.

Writing $\rcnts$ for the multiset of counts of the reads at $(a,v)$, balance therefore says $\gen\cdot\rcnts=\rcnts$. There are fewer than $2^{64}-1$ of them, so Lemma~\ref{lem:invcnt} makes every count in $\rcnts$ zero; every count is nonzero by hypothesis, so $\rcnts$ is empty. No read happens at such an invalid $(a,v)$.

\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Addresses are in range]\label{cor:addrrange}
Under the same hypotheses every read address is an entry address $\addr_i$, $i<2^{\kappa}$: no read at any other address, $0$ included, occurs in an accepted proof (except with negligible soundness error).
\end{corollary}

\paragraph{Nothing checks the finalize counts.} $\cntfin$ appears nowhere in Theorem~\ref{thm:omc} or its proof. A wrong value (such as $0$ for instance) cannot make a bad read pass; it can only unbalance the bus at that entry, making the proof invalid.

\paragraph{Meeting the hypotheses.} Grand-product GKR checks balance (\S\ref{sec:gp}). It remains to check that all counts are nonzero, and the $\nread<2^{64}-1$ read bound, as follows.

\paragraph{The count product.} All counts are nonzero exactly when their product is. The prover therefore sends that product, one $\E$-element, and the verifier checks it is nonzero. It is proven like any other grand product, by GKR over the stacking of every count column (\S\ref{sec:leafstack}), batched with the two other GKRs of the bus (push / pull) (\S\ref{sec:gkr}).

\paragraph{Instance caps.} The verifier checks the announced sizes against public caps (\S\ref{sec:e2e-unrolled}): at most $2^{32}$ rows per table, and the memory map's bounds on the text, RAM and the advice (\S\ref{sec:vm}). A row reads a lookup array once, its bytecode entry, so the eight tables flush $\nread\le8\cdot2^{32}=2^{35}$ reads, far under the $2^{64}-1$ that Lemma~\ref{lem:invcnt} requires.

\paragraph{Relaxing the hypotheses.} A bus entry may be any polynomial of degree at most $d = 2$ in the row's columns (\S\ref{sec:m3}), so none of the three (address, count, value) need be committed. Example 1: once the pull counter $\cnt$ is committed, no need to commit to the corresponding push counter $\gen\cdot\cnt$. Example 2: the next $\pc$ a branch pushes is $\pc^{+}+b\cdot\Delta$, a product of two committed columns added to a third, and no column of its own (\S\ref{sec:tables}).

\subsection{Read-write arrays}\label{sec:memchan}

Three arrays are read-write: the registers $\regs$, $2^{6}$ cells (\S\ref{sec:vm}), under $\dsep{REG}=\gen^{3}$; RAM $\mem$, $\nmem=2^{\kmem}$ cells, and the advice $\adv$, $2^{\kadv}$ cells, both under $\dsep{MEM}=\gen^{1}$, which they can share because a RAM address and an advice address never coincide (\S\ref{sec:vm}). The lookup protocol of \S\ref{sec:lookup} does not apply to them, because what a cell holds depends on when it is read. The memory argument orders the accesses instead, by the timestamps the clock of \S\ref{sec:state} gives them. The tuple is $\tup{\dsep{sep},\addr,\mathit{timestamp},\val}$, and everything below is said of one array at a time; a register's address is its number, $\intk{r}$, a memory cell's its byte address.

\paragraph{Columns.} Per array, two $\K$-valued multilinears of its height are committed: what it holds after the run, $\regfin$, $\memfin$ or $\advfin$, and each cell's last timestamp, $\tsfin$. What it holds before the run is public for the registers (zero) and for RAM ($\meminit$: the image, then zeros, a column the verifier evaluates itself, \S\ref{sec:e2e-pi}), and committed for the advice ($\advinit$), which is the prover's. An access is part of a row of a table. Besides the row's clock $\ts$, it takes $\tsprev$, the timestamp of the cell's previous access; a write takes one more, the value $v_{\mathrm{old}}$ the cell held. Neither $\ts$ nor $\tsprev$ is a committed column: both are ports of the row's clock circuit (\S\ref{sec:clock}). The address $a$ is a column of the row as well, though seldom a committed one (\S\ref{sec:exp}).

\paragraph{Flush rules.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Seed:} for each cell $i$, \push\ $\tup{\dsep{sep},\addr_i,\intk{2^{40}},\mathrm{init}[i]}$, with $\mathrm{init}$ the array's initial column, zero, $\meminit$ or $\advinit$: the live timestamp of cycle $0$.
\item \textbf{Access} of the cell at $a$, in slot $k$ of a row with clock $\ts$: \pull\ $\tup{\dsep{sep},a,\tsprev,v_{\mathrm{old}}}$ and \push\ $\tup{\dsep{sep},a,\ts+\intk{k},v_{\mathrm{new}}}$. A read is an access with $v_{\mathrm{new}}=v_{\mathrm{old}}$, one column serving as both.
\item \textbf{Finalize:} for each $i$, \pull\ $\tup{\dsep{sep},\addr_i,\tsfin[i],\mathrm{fin}[i]}$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The order check.} The row's clock circuit checks, for each access, with $\ell$ the live bit of $\ts$ and every timestamp read as an integer below $2^{41}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:order}
  \tsprev_{40}=\ell,\qquad\text{and, if }\ell=1,\qquad \tsprev<\ts+k ,
\end{equation}
and it puts the verdict in bit $41$ of the row's next clock (\S\ref{sec:clock}), so by Corollary~\ref{cor:clock} every access of an accepted proof satisfies it. The strictness is essential: were $\tsprev=\ts+k$ allowed, a read could pull the very tuple it pushes, and return any value. The live bits are what keep a padding row away from the run: equal on both sides of an access, they let no padding row pull a seed, and no live row pull a padding row's tuple (\S\ref{sec:e2e-pad}).

\paragraph{Completeness.} An honest prover gives every access the timestamp its cell was last accessed at, $\intk{2^{40}}$ for a first access, and commits $\tsfin$ and the final column accordingly. Slots are numbered in the order a row performs its accesses, reads first, so two accesses of one row to the same cell are in order too: a row reads $\regs[\mathit{rs1}]$ in slot $0$ and $\regs[\mathit{rs2}]$ in slot $1$, makes its memory accesses from slot $2$, and writes $\regs[\mathit{rd}]$ in slot $3$, a row that skips an access leaving its slot unused (\S\ref{sec:tables}). What is required is that the run stay below $2^{35}-1$ cycles (Corollary~\ref{cor:clock}).

\begin{theorem}[Memory consistency]\label{thm:rwmem}
Fix one array. Let the flush rules above be the only flushes carrying its separator at its addresses, let the clocks be as in Corollary~\ref{cor:clock}, and let every access satisfy \eqref{eq:order}. If the bus balances, then every access of a row with a live clock is at an address $\addr_i$ of the array, and, the accesses of such rows at $\addr_i$ being taken in the order of their timestamps: the first one's $v_{\mathrm{old}}$ is $\mathrm{init}[i]$, every other's $v_{\mathrm{old}}$ is its predecessor's $v_{\mathrm{new}}$, and $\mathrm{fin}[i]$ is the last one's $v_{\mathrm{new}}$, or $\mathrm{init}[i]$ if there is none.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Call an access \emph{live} if its row's clock is. Its pushed timestamp $\ts+\intk{k}$ then has the live bit, and so has its $\tsprev$, by \eqref{eq:order}. For any other access neither does. So split the tuples of the channel by whether their timestamp has the live bit. The bus balances within each class, the seeds and the live accesses lie in the live one, and the rest of the proof takes place there. The other class holds the other accesses, and proves nothing.

By Corollary~\ref{cor:clock} the live rows are the walk $r_1,\dots,r_L$, with clocks $2^{40}+2^{5}i$, and a row's slots are below $2^{5}$, so the live accesses have pairwise distinct timestamps $y$, all above the seeds' $2^{40}$. For one of them, \eqref{eq:order} gives $\tsprev=\intk{x}$ with $x<y$.

Fix an address $a$ and keep the live tuples at $a$, which still balance. If some live access is at $a$, the one of smallest $y$ pulls a timestamp $x<y$, which no live access at $a$ pushed: the matching push is a seed, so $a=\addr_i$ for a cell $i$ of the array. At such an address, let the live accesses have timestamps $y_1<\dots<y_n$. With $y_0:=2^{40}$, the pushes carry the $n+1$ distinct timestamps $\intk{y_0},\intk{y_1},\dots,\intk{y_n}$, so the pulls are $n+1$ as well, which puts the finalization in this class, and carry the same ones: $\intk{x_1},\dots,\intk{x_n}$ and $\tsfin[i]$ are those $n+1$ values in some order. By induction on $j$, the only $y_l$ below $y_j$ not already taken is $y_{j-1}$; so $x_j=y_{j-1}$, which leaves $\tsfin[i]=\intk{y_n}$. A pull and the push it matches agree on the value, which is the claim.
\end{proof}

RAM and the advice share $\dsep{MEM}$, and the theorem applies to each: a tuple at a RAM address is never one at an advice address, so keeping the tuples at one array's addresses is the split the proof makes. What the theorem also says is that an access outside every array, a misaligned one included (\S\ref{sec:exp}), has no matching seed, so the bus does not balance: no range check on addresses is needed.

\paragraph{Nothing checks the final columns.} As with $\cntfin$, a wrong $\tsfin$ or final column cannot make a bad access pass: it can only unbalance the bus. The same holds for $\advinit$, which is the prover's to choose (\S\ref{sec:vm}).

\subsection{The clock circuit}\label{sec:clock}

Beside its class's circuit, each table has a second circuit in the sense of Annex~\ref{annex:flock}, its \emph{clock circuit}, one instance per row, packed into a witness $\qclk{j}$ of its own. Its input ports are the row's clock $\ts$ and the previous timestamp $\tsprev_k$ of each of its accesses, and its one output port is $\mathit{step}$; each is a $\K$-element as it stands, like a class circuit's ports, so they are virtual columns of the table (\S\ref{sec:tab-class}). An input port has free wires at its $41$ low positions only, its high ones being empty, hence zero (\S\ref{flock:classes}): every clock and every previous timestamp a row pulls is below $2^{41}$.

Write $\ell=\ts_{40}$, and $y_k$ for $\ts$ with its five low bits replaced by those of the slot $k$, which is $\ts+k$ on every live row (Corollary~\ref{cor:clock}). For an access in slot $k$ the circuit computes the carry out of position $39$ of $y_k+\overline{\tsprev}$, over positions $0$ to $39$, with the adder of \S\ref{flock:classes}: one $\wedge$ wire per position, fewer at the low positions, where $y_k$'s bits are constants. It is set exactly when $\tsprev<y_k$ modulo $2^{40}$, so, with the live bits equal, exactly when $\tsprev<\ts+k$ on a live row. The verdict is
\[
  \mathit{fail}=\ell\wedge\neg\bigwedge_k \mathit{carry}_k\;\vee\;\bigvee_k\,(\tsprev_{k,40}\oplus\ell).
\]
Then $\mathit{step}$ holds $\ell$ at position $5$, at position $j+1$ for $5\le j<40$ the carry $c_{j+1}=\ts_j\,c_j$ into position $j+1$ of $\ts+2^{5}\ell$, with $c_5=\ell$, and $\mathit{fail}$ at position $41$. So $\ts+\mathit{step}$ is $\intk{(\ts+2^{5}\ell)\bmod2^{41}}$ when every access is in order, and that clock with bit $41$ set otherwise, which is the next clock of \S\ref{sec:state}. The carries are products placed straight at $\mathit{step}$'s positions, so the output costs two copies and no position of its own.

\subsection{Bytecode}\label{sec:bytecode}

The bytecode is a lookup array (\S\ref{sec:lookup}) of $\nprog = 2^{\kbc}$ entries, entry $i$ at the address $\intk{\tbase+4i}$, the byte address of the instruction. Bus interaction uses domain separation $\sep=\dsep{BC} = \gen^{2}$. The program is public, and so is its decoding: an entry is not the instruction word but the fields a row needs of it, decoded once by both parties (\S\ref{sec:e2e-bc}). So $\ncomp=10$: the class tag, the flags, the three register numbers, the immediate, the successor $\intk{\tbase+4i+4}$, which every row pushes as its next $\pc$ unless it jumps, and the three control fields of a branch or a jump. With the separator, the address and the count ahead of them, this is the widest entry on the bus at $13$ coordinates, which is what forces the four index bits, hence the $m=16$ slots of \S\ref{sec:m3}; the row of a division puts one more word in slot $13$ (\S\ref{sec:tables}). A jump's target needs no check of its own: the row it leads to reads the bytecode there, and Corollary~\ref{cor:addrrange} makes it an instruction. Coordinate $3$ is the tag, naming the instruction's class and its table:
\begin{gather*}
  \opc{ALU}=\gen^{0},\quad \opc{LOAD}=\gen^{1},\quad \opc{STORE}=\gen^{2},\quad \opc{SHIFT}=\gen^{3},\\
  \opc{MUL}=\gen^{4},\quad \opc{MULH}=\gen^{5},\quad \opc{DIV}=\gen^{6},\quad \opc{HASH}=\gen^{7}.
\end{gather*}
A slot of the text that holds no instruction the machine defines has the tag $0$, which is no table's: a run reaching it has no proof.

\subsection{Addresses}\label{sec:exp}

Integer addition is no low-degree operation of $\K$, and none is needed on the bus. A register's address is its number, a field of the public bytecode entry. A load's or a store's address is $\intk{\regs[\mathit{rs1}]+\mathrm{imm}}$, and computing it is the business of the row's circuit (\S\ref{sec:tables}), which also puts the misalignment bits back into the word it hands the bus: an aligned access names its cell's address, a misaligned one an address no cell has, so Theorem~\ref{thm:rwmem} refuses it with no constraint of its own. The words of a \texttt{blake2s} block are at $\regs[\mathit{rs1}]\oplus8k$ by definition (\S\ref{sec:vm}), and the field sum $\intk{\regs[\mathit{rs1}]}+\intk{8k}$ is that XOR: the address is a column plus a constant.

\subsection{The index columns}\label{sec:idxcol}

The seed and finalization blocks run over every entry of their array, so the verifier needs the extensions of the address columns at the point $\zeta$ (\S\ref{sec:leafstack}). Write $w$ for the bits of $i$. Every region's cell $i$ sits at $\mathrm{base}\oplus(i\ll\sigma)$ with $\sigma=3$ for the words of RAM and the advice, $2$ for the instructions and $0$ for the registers (base $0$), and this \emph{integer index column} is affine in the bits, $\intk{\mathrm{base}}+\sum_k w_k\,x^{k+\sigma}$, so its extension is that same sum at $\zeta$:
\[
  \intk{\mathrm{base}}+\sum_{k=0}^{\kappa-1}\zeta_k\,x^{k+\sigma}.
\]
